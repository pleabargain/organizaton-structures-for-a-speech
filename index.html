Glossary of organizational structures

Use these definitions while you take the quiz. Close the glossary to return exactly where you left off.

Problem/Solution

A Problem/Solution (also called problem-solution) speech is organized around a need and a remedy. First you make the audience understand that something is wrong, incomplete, or costly. Then you present one or more practical responses and show why they address that need.

This structure works well when your purpose is to persuade people to adopt a policy, process, product, or habit. Strong versions usually define the problem with evidence, propose a clear solution, and explain benefits or implementation—not merely complain.

Example cue: “Guests stop coming after one visit → propose a buddy system.” The organizing spine is need, then fix.

Chronological

A chronological speech arranges ideas in time order: past to present, beginning to end, step one to step last. The audience follows a sequence of events, stages, or developments as they unfolded (or as they should unfold).

Use chronology for histories, biographies, process how-tos, and “how we got here” stories. The key test: if you reshuffled the sections, the speech would feel out of order because time is the logic of the outline.

Example cue: “Invention → historical impact → modern role.” Dates and eras drive the structure.

Topical

A topical speech divides the subject into natural categories, themes, or types. Each main point is a distinct “bucket” of content—genres, features, stakeholder groups, product lines—rather than a timeline, a map, or a cause chain.

Topical organization is flexible and common. Choose it when your material clusters into parallel sections that could be presented in several orders without breaking the logic (even if you still pick a sensible order for emphasis).

Example cue: “Three dessert styles—custards, cakes, and tarts—with examples under each.” Categories, not time, organize the talk.

Spatial

A spatial speech organizes content by physical location, geography, or layout: left to right, floor to floor, coast to mountain, base camp to summit. Listeners move through space in their minds as you move through your points.

Choose spatial structure for tours, facility descriptions, geographic surveys, and any talk where place is the clearest way to group information. If elevation zones, rooms, or map regions are the outline, you are using spatial organization.

Example cue: “What happens to the body at different elevations on a mountain.” Position in space is the organizing principle.

Causal

A causal speech focuses on cause and effect. You explain why something happens, what results from a condition, or how a chain of influences produces an outcome (A leads to B leads to C).

This structure fits scientific claims, risk explanations, and “if we do X, Y follows” arguments. The outline is driven by relationships of influence—not by calendar order alone, not by place, and not by listing unrelated categories.

Example cue: “Regular exercise contributes to better overall health.” Cause → effect is the thesis and the structure.

Comparative

A comparative speech places two or more options, systems, or cases side by side to highlight similarities and differences. Shared criteria (cost, quality, culture, emissions) usually guide the comparison so the audience can judge fairly.

Example cue: “Similarities and differences between two countries’ economic structures.”

Particular / General / Particular

This pattern starts with a specific example or story (particular), expands to a broader principle that applies more widely (general), then returns to the specific case to illustrate or resolve it (particular again). It helps audiences feel a concrete moment, understand a rule, and reconnect emotionally with the original story.

Example cue: “One workplace morning → what positive attitude does for any team → return to that morning’s outcome.”

Why this method works

This quiz uses retrieval practice, immediate explanatory feedback, short return after misses, and longer cooldowns after mastery. Below is the plain-language rationale with research links you can open.

Immediate feedback, then Next

As soon as you choose an option, the quiz locks your answer, marks correct or incorrect, and shows a short reason. You click Next when ready.

  • After an error, feedback matters so the wrong choice does not linger uncorrected. Unsuccessful retrieval attempts are not inherently harmful when feedback follows—and can even help you encode the right answer afterward (Kornell, Hays, & Bjork, 2009 PDF; Pashler et al., 2003 PDF).
  • Feedback with useful information beats bare grades (Wisniewski, Zierer, & Hattie, 2020).

Short return after incorrect answers (short cooldown)

If you miss a structure, the quiz prioritizes it and does not bury it behind a long cooldown. Similar questions tend to return sooner.

  • Expanding retrieval starts with relatively short intervals when memory is fragile, then stretches gaps after success (Landauer & Bjork, 1978 PDF).
  • Distributed-practice reviews show that weaker learning generally needs closer follow-up than mastered material (Cepeda et al., 2006; PubMed record).
  • In plain terms: do not punish a miss with a long wait—correct it, then practice it again soon.

Longer cooldown after correct streaks

If you keep answering a structure correctly, the quiz cools it down for several upcoming questions and spends time elsewhere.

  • The spacing / lag effect: spreading practice improves later retention more than massing easy successes (Cepeda et al., 2006).
  • Desirable difficulties—including spacing and interleaving—often improve long-term learning even when practice feels harder in the moment (Bjork overview PDF; open-access discussion: PMC6289840).
  • Related open-access work on retrieval practice and spacing: PMC4480221.
